# Color and Relaxation

Physiological and psychological calming effects of color, with emphasis on blue
and green.

## Blue: fastest stress recovery

### Physiological evidence

Blue lighting accelerated post-stress relaxation by 3x in a preliminary EEG/ECG
study (N=12). Participants reached minimum stress levels in ~1.1 minutes under
blue light vs. ~3.5 minutes under white light. 83% subjectively reported
feeling more relaxed. However, "the advantage of blue lighting disappeared"
after 3.5–5 minutes due to sensory adaptation (source:
minguillon-2017-blue-lighting-relaxation.md).

Blue viewing can lower heart rate and blood pressure. A Sleep Foundation survey
found people with blue bedrooms reported the longest average sleep duration
compared to any other bedroom color (source:
scienceinsights-colors-relieve-stress.md).

### Mechanism

Light blue activates the parasympathetic nervous system — the branch
responsible for rest and recovery. This is a measurable physiological response,
not merely a subjective association (source:
cognifit-colors-that-calm-the-mind.md).

### Timing caveat

Bright blue light in evening hours triggers cortisol increases when the body
should be relaxing. The same wavelength that aids morning alertness disrupts
evening wind-down. Short-wavelength blue light (470–480 nm) helps reset cortisol
rhythms in the morning but should be avoided at night (source:
scienceinsights-colors-relieve-stress.md).

## Green: nature-connected calm

### Physiological evidence

Research on Scottish urban populations showed that people living in
neighborhoods with more green space had lower perceived stress and healthier
cortisol patterns (source: scienceinsights-colors-relieve-stress.md).

Exposure to green environments lowers heart rate, blood pressure, and muscle
tension. Walking in green environments induces significant heart rate reduction
compared to red and white conditions (source:
neurolaunch-green-color-psychology.md).

### Cognitive restoration

Green exposure restores mental energy after demanding cognitive tasks and
improves working memory. The mechanism is Attention Restoration Theory: natural
green stimuli engage involuntary attention, allowing directed attention to
recover (source: cognifit-colors-that-calm-the-mind.md,
berto-2022-green-school-environment-cognition.md).

### Shade matters

Soft shades (sage, muted olive, dusty green) work better than vivid tones for
relaxation. See [[green-shades]] for the full breakdown (source:
scienceinsights-colors-relieve-stress.md).

## The saturation principle

The most important practical finding: **low-saturation, moderate-brightness
cool colors are calming; high-saturation versions of the same hues are
arousing.** A muted warm color can be more calming than a saturated cool color.
The optimal relaxation palette uses cool hues with low saturation and
moderate-to-low brightness (source: scienceinsights-colors-relieve-stress.md).

## Cortisol reduction

Sustained exposure to calming color environments (low-saturation blues, greens,
lavenders) can diminish cortisol levels. The relationship between color
environment and cortisol patterns suggests cumulative health benefits from
long-term exposure (source: scienceinsights-colors-relieve-stress.md,
cognifit-colors-that-calm-the-mind.md).
