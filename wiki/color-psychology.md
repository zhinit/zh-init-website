# Color Psychology

The study of how color perception influences cognition, emotion, and behavior.
Distinct from [[color-theory]], which covers color selection and combination for
UI design. This page covers the empirical research on what colors do to people.

## Theoretical foundations

### Color-in-Context Theory

The dominant framework (Elliot & Maier, 2012). Color effects depend on
context — red might increase attraction on a date but trigger anxiety during a
test. Responses reflect both biological predispositions and learned
associations. "Blue on a ribbon is positive (first place), but blue on meat is
negative (rotten)" (source: elliot-2015-color-psychological-functioning-review.md).

### Wavelength-dependent arousal

Blue light activates the melanopsin photoreceptor system, enhancing alertness.
This is a hardwired biological response, not a cultural association (source:
elliot-2015-color-psychological-functioning-review.md).

### Attention Restoration Theory (ART)

Natural (green) environments engage involuntary attention through soft stimuli,
allowing directed attention to recover from fatigue. This explains why green
exposure restores cognitive performance after demanding tasks (source:
berto-2022-green-school-environment-cognition.md).

### Stress Reduction Theory (SRT)

Green spaces activate parasympathetic nervous system responses, reducing
physiological stress. The effect is measurable through heart rate, blood
pressure, and cortisol levels (source:
berto-2022-green-school-environment-cognition.md).

## Saturation matters more than hue

A 2017 study found that "saturated, bright colors triggered significantly
stronger arousal responses regardless of hue." A vivid blue energizes; powder
blue relaxes. A neon green stimulates; sage green calms. The optimal
combination for relaxation is cool colors with low saturation and
moderate-to-low brightness (source: scienceinsights-colors-relieve-stress.md).

This is the single most important finding for applying color psychology: shade
and saturation determine the psychological effect more than the base hue.

## Methodological caveats

Elliot's 2015 review identifies serious weaknesses in the field: inadequate
spectral-level color control, underpowered samples, excessive focus on red,
and insufficient analysis of when effects occur (not just whether). "The
existing theoretical and empirical work is at an early stage of development"
(source: elliot-2015-color-psychological-functioning-review.md).

Findings below are suggestive, not settled science.
