# Font Psychology

The study of how typefaces influence perception, emotion, and behavior. Font
psychology sits at the intersection of cognitive science, design, and
marketing — a young field with a growing body of experimental evidence.

## Core mechanisms

### Cognitive fluency

The ease with which the brain processes information directly affects perceived
truthfulness. Schwarz and Reber demonstrated that participants judged
statements as significantly more true when displayed in high-contrast,
easy-to-read typography versus low-contrast, hard-to-read versions. Clear,
familiar fonts enhance perceived truthfulness because they require less mental
effort (source: cognitiontoday-font-psychology-research.md).

Implication: legibility is not just a UX concern — it is a trust concern.

### The disfluency effect

Paradoxically, hard-to-read fonts can improve memory retention. Diemand-Yauman
et al. (2011) found that information in harder-to-read fonts was remembered
better, and participants reading Monotype Corsiva demonstrated superior recall
compared to simple font readers. Hard-to-read fonts also reduce
distractibility in noisy environments (source:
cognitiontoday-font-psychology-research.md, todaymade-font-psychology-guide.md).

This does not contradict cognitive fluency — fluency aids trust and
believability, disfluency aids memorization. Different goals, different tools.

### Neural pathways

Neuroimaging studies show different fonts activate brain areas associated with
emotion processing. The responses to typography have neurological foundations,
not just cultural ones (source: todaymade-font-psychology-guide.md).

## Key studies

### Monotype emotion study (2022)

400 participants evaluated three typefaces (Cotford serif, FS Jack humanist
sans, Gilroy geometric sans) across individual words, sentences, and branded
sentences. Results (source: monotype-font-emotion-study-2022.md):

| Typeface | Category | Key finding |
|----------|----------|-------------|
| Cotford | Serif | Trustworthiness +9%, memorability +10%, quality perception increased |
| FS Jack | Humanist sans | Sincerity +10%, honesty +5% |
| Gilroy | Geometric sans | Prominence +12% (bold), competitor standout +5% |

Font selection boosted consumer response by up to 13% — exceeding the
researchers' expected 0–5% range.

FS Jack's warmth was attributed to specific letterform features: open
terminals, squarer curves, double-storey a and g.

### IBM Design experiment (2016)

73 participants from 17 countries evaluated four typefaces (Baskerville, Fira
Sans, Helvetica, Roboto Slab) across four website types (banking, news,
fitness, clothing). Findings (source:
ibm-design-fonts-influence-perception-2016.md):

- Baskerville ranked highest overall for trustworthiness
- Sans-serif fonts (Fira, Helvetica) performed better for "easy to use"
- Context mattered: Fira was most trustworthy on a fitness site (opposite of
  the news site result)
- "Appeal" differences between fonts were not statistically significant

Conclusion: typography influences each product differently — there is no
universally "best" font.

### Errol Morris / New York Times experiment (2013)

Readers showed greater agreement with essays displayed in Baskerville compared
to Comic Sans and Helvetica. The effect was subconscious (source:
ibm-design-fonts-influence-perception-2016.md).

### Shaikh & Chaparro typeface personality study

379 participants rated 40 typefaces on 15 semantic differential scales. Factor
analysis revealed three dimensions of typeface personality (source:
designmodo-font-psychology.md, todaymade-font-psychology-guide.md):

1. **Potency** — ruggedness, masculinity
2. **Evaluative** — perceived beauty and value
3. **Activity** — excitement, loudness, speed

Results by category:
- Serif and sans-serif typefaces were neutral across all three factors
- Bold, blocky display typefaces were perceived as more Potent and more
  Evaluative
- Script typefaces were perceived as less Potent, more Evaluative, and less
  Active
- Serif fonts scored highest on "stable, practical, mature, formal"

### Juni & Gross (2008)

A satirical passage in Times New Roman was perceived as funnier and angrier
than the same passage in Arial — demonstrating that typeface modulates
emotional interpretation of identical content (source:
cognitiontoday-font-psychology-research.md).

## Practical implications

The Monotype study's 13% response boost and the IBM study's context-dependent
results point to the same conclusion: font choice has measurable impact, but
the "right" font depends on the specific context, audience, and medium. There
is no universal answer.

What the research does establish:
- Serifs signal tradition, authority, trust
- Humanist sans-serifs signal warmth, sincerity, honesty
- Geometric sans-serifs signal modernity, prominence
- Legibility drives trust (cognitive fluency)
- Difficulty drives memorability (disfluency effect)
